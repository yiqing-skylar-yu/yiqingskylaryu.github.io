Mentoring

Mentoring Philosophy

I want research assistants to leave the lab with more than completed hours. Their experience should build research skills, confidence, career clarity, and increasing independence. Because students join research teams for different reasons, I tailor opportunities to their goals rather than assuming that every student wants an academic career.

Students interested in research-intensive careers may progress toward literature review, analysis, conference presentations, and manuscript writing, while others may emphasize communication, teamwork, project management, participant interaction, and scientific literacy.

From Training to Independence

I tailor students’ research experiences to their goals, interests, and level of experience. Early conversations about why they joined the lab, what skills they want to develop, and what they hope to do next help us identify responsibilities and opportunities that fit their goals. From there, my mentoring follows a developmental progression: students begin with clear structure, gain autonomy as their skills grow, and can eventually take on leadership and ownership within the research team.

Start with Structure

New research assistants begin with detailed written protocols, mock sessions, research-ethics guidance, clear expectations for documentation and reliability, competency checks, and close feedback. This structure gives students a dependable foundation for learning while protecting the quality of the research.

Build Independence

As students gain experience, the goal shifts from simply following procedures to understanding why those procedures matter. Students learn to troubleshoot problems, connect day-to-day research tasks with study design and data quality, and make increasingly independent decisions within their roles.

Create Leadership Opportunities

Advanced students may take on roles in project coordination, recruitment, data quality, peer training, science communication, or project leadership. These roles give experienced students opportunities to mentor peers, improve workflows, monitor research quality, and take ownership of meaningful parts of a project.

Weekly Lab Meetings as a Research Seminar

Weekly lab meetings function as a research seminar rather than only an operations meeting. We discuss assigned papers, student-generated questions, study design, methods, data-quality issues, conference presentations, and manuscripts. I also use short methods lessons and applied exercises to expose students to research skills they may not encounter in their coursework.

For example, students might code a short interview excerpt while learning about qualitative methods or use a meta-analysis paper to discuss how researchers synthesize evidence across studies.

Learning the Research Lifecycle

Students may enter a project during recruitment or data collection, but I want them to understand how research develops from an idea into a study, dataset, conference presentation, and publication. Depending on their interests and time in the lab, students may contribute to literature review, protocol development, recruitment, data collection, quality control, analysis, dissemination, or writing.

Mentee trajectory: One of my undergraduate research mentees began on my team running study sessions and later contributed to literature review, study planning, project coordination, conference presentations, and manuscript writing. Our mentoring relationship continued after she left CSU and during her time at Brown University as a full-time research coordinator, and she is now pursuing a Ph.D. in Clinical Psychology.

Evidence of Mentoring Effectiveness

Across 61 mentoring evaluations collected from 2022–2026, students rated their overall research experience an average of 4.79/5; among those completing the mentor-specific item, my average overall mentor rating was 4.91/5.

“I have learned so much being in this lab. I felt like I was able to grow a lot personally and academically.”

Students also frequently describe gaining experience with study design, protocol administration, participant interaction, literature searching, data collection, research ethics, troubleshooting, and scientific communication.

Mentoring Other Mentors

My interest in mentoring extends beyond my own research team. I have given invited and conference presentations on graduate-student mentorship and undergraduate RA development, co-authored a Society of Behavioral Medicine article on effective mentorship, and contribute to mentor training and evaluation through CSU’s Graduate School.
