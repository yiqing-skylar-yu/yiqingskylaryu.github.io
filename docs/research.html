Research

Making Healthy Behavior Fit Real Life

My research asks how health-promoting behaviors can fit into people’s everyday lives rather than compete with them. I focus primarily on physical activity and sedentary behavior, studying how timing, task demands, individual differences, and everyday context shape whether an intervention is usable and effective.

Across this work, I return to one question: When, for whom, and under what conditions does an intervention work?

Movement In Everyday Life

I study how light physical activity can be integrated into desk-based work and other everyday settings without disrupting performance or making everyday tasks harder.

Individual and Contextual Fit

The same intervention can help one person, burden another, and make little difference for someone else. I study how individual characteristics, task demands, timing, and context shape those differences.

Sustaining Healthy Behavior

I am interested in what happens after an intervention initially works: whether people keep using it, what helps healthy behavior become part of a routine, and how sustained behavior affects health and well-being.

From Laboratory to Everyday Life

I use controlled laboratory studies to isolate mechanisms and test causal questions, then field and mixed-methods studies to understand whether the same ideas remain useful in everyday settings.

The questions move in both directions. Practical barriers observed in the field inform new laboratory studies, while laboratory findings help identify what should be tested at larger scale.

Mechanism → Feasibility → Field effectiveness → Sustained use → Refined mechanism

This iterative approach lets practical constraints become part of the science rather than a problem discovered only after an intervention leaves the laboratory.

Representative Projects

Activity-Permissive Workstations, Performance, and User Experience

Can people walk while working without compromising the work itself? In a randomized study comparing sitting, standing, and treadmill walking, cognitive performance was similar across workstation conditions, although walking participants perceived typing and mouse use as more difficult. They also reported greater enjoyment and less boredom. The findings suggest that the usefulness of treadmill desks depends more on the task than on a simple “good versus bad” judgment.

Walking While Working Under Stress

Does walking remain workable when work becomes stressful and cognitively demanding? In a preregistered experiment, walking during an acute social-evaluative stressor did not buffer stress responses, but it also did not amplify perceived stress or workload. Walking participants rated the workstation as more enjoyable than sitting and more comfortable than standing. This work also shows why physiological responses during active work need to be interpreted against a movement-specific baseline.

Dissertation: When Should We Walk Around Stress?

My dissertation tests whether a brief walk works differently when it occurs before, during, or after an acute stressor. Preliminary quantitative findings do not suggest one universally optimal timing, while participants’ qualitative accounts reveal substantial differences in whether walking feels like preparation, coping, distraction, added demand, or recovery. I am using those differences to study how intervention timing and individual experience shape intervention fit.

Task-Specific Treadmill-Desk Feasibility

Not every office task is equally compatible with walking. This mixed-methods project examines which everyday tasks people are willing and able to complete on a treadmill desk, and how task demands, ergonomics, pace, social context, and user control shape that experience.

Longitudinal Treadmill-Desk Field Pilot

What determines whether someone continues using a treadmill desk once the novelty wears off? This year-long daily-diary pilot follows self-directed treadmill-desk use among administrative staff, examining use patterns, barriers, perceived benefits, and day-to-day context. The project is informing larger longitudinal studies of sustained movement at work.

Health Literacy and Menu Labeling

A complementary line of my research examines whether health information works equally well for different people. In a national sample of U.S. adults, I found substantial sociodemographic differences in functional health literacy. In a randomized menu-labeling study, nutrition labels were associated with healthier ordering primarily among adults with moderate health literacy rather than uniformly across literacy groups. Together, this work reinforces a broader theme of my research: population-level interventions can produce uneven effects depending on how well they fit the people expected to use them.

Methodological Approach

I use experiments, mixed methods, psychophysiology, qualitative interviews, and intensive longitudinal field designs. I choose methods around the question, often combining controlled tests of causal mechanisms with measures of feasibility, individual differences, and real-world use.

Where This Work Is Going

My next studies will extend this program from short-term feasibility toward sustained behavior change in everyday settings. I am particularly interested in comparing different ways of integrating movement into work, identifying person-by-context differences in response, and using repeated-measures and wearable-supported field studies to understand when healthy behavior becomes sustainable.

More broadly, I want to develop health interventions that fit people’s existing lives rather than requiring people to reorganize their lives around the intervention.
